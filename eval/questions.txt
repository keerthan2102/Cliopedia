# Cliopedia's retrieval question set.
#
# Each entry is a question plus the page numbers KNOWN to answer it. Running
# `python -m cliopedia.evaluate` asks each question and checks whether one of
# those pages came back in the top k. That is the retrieval hit rate — the one
# number that says whether the search half of this system actually works.
#
#
# ── The format ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
#
#   ask:    the question, phrased as a student would ask it
#   book:   which book the gold pages were read from — a corpus key
#   pages:  printed page numbers, comma separated
#   why:    how you verified it — free text, any characters, any length
#
# A blank line ends an entry. Lines starting with # are ignored.
#
# There are NO quoting rules. Paste anything into `why`: quotation marks,
# apostrophes, dashes, text that wraps across five lines. A continuation line
# is simply any line that does not begin with `ask:`, `book:`, `pages:` or
# `why:`. This file is written by hand, so it is designed not to break when you
# type in it.
#
# `book` is one of the keys declared in cliopedia/corpus.py — currently
# `us-history`, `world-history-1`, `world-history-2`. It is REQUIRED, and an
# unknown key is rejected rather than ignored.
#
#
# ── Why `book` is not optional ──────────────────────────────────────────────
#
# Every volume in this corpus has a page 210. While the corpus was one book a
# page number identified a passage on its own; with three it does not. Scoring
# on the page alone, a World History page 210 returned for a U.S. History
# question counts as a HIT — the wrong book, scored correct.
#
# Note which way that error runs. It makes the number go UP. A gold page that
# is simply wrong shows up as a miss and provokes an investigation; a wrong-book
# hit shows up as success and provokes nothing. So the book is written down
# next to the pages, per entry, where it can be read.
#
#
# ── The trap to avoid ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────
#
# Do NOT write questions by running retrieval and recording what comes back.
# That measures whether retrieval agrees with itself, which it always will, and
# the score would be meaningless.
#
# Write them the other way round: open the PDF at a page, READ it, and write a
# question that page answers. The gold page is then the page you read, chosen
# independently of anything the system does.
#
# `pages` are PRINTED page numbers — the folio typeset on the page, not the
# PDF page. The app reports printed pages, so the two compare directly.
#
# The offset between the two is per book, because it comes from that book's own
# front matter. For U.S. History it is 16, so PDF page 226 is printed page 210.
# Do not carry that 16 across to another volume — read each book's own offset
# off its pages, or let ingest print it.
#
# Spread the pages across the whole book. Twenty questions clustered in one
# chapter measure one chapter.
#
# Worth including on purpose: a few questions whose answer spans two pages
# (list both, either counts), and a few asked in a student's words rather than
# the textbook's — matching paraphrase is the whole reason for using embeddings
# instead of keyword search.

ask: Why did people at Çatalhöyük enter their houses through the roof?
book: world-history-1
pages: 61
why: Read printed page 61: "Houses at Çatalhöyük were made with mud brick and
  were clustered together without roads or passages between them. This design
  required that residents enter their homes from the roof, but it provided them
  with protection from the outside world."

ask: How many workers did it take to build the Great Pyramid at Giza?
book: world-history-1
pages: 96
why: Read printed page 96: Herodotus "recorded that the pyramid of Khufu took
  100,000 workers twenty years to construct", but "Modern archaeologists suspect
  that a much smaller but still substantial workforce of around twenty thousand
  was likely employed." Chosen partly because the page gives two competing
  figures — a good test of whether the retrieved passage supports the answer.

  by his own crew, then he was refused the cedar he was promised in Phoenicia,

  Late Bronze Age Collapse.

ask: How did the Hundred Years War contribute to the decline of feudalism?
book: world-history-1
pages: 690
why: Read printed page 690: the war "changed the nature of warfare and minimized
  the nobles' traditional military role", because "large professional standing
  armies paid for by monarchs could defeat mounted knights with the use of the
  longbow", and "the combination of depopulation, shifting military practices,
  and centralization of monarchical power led to the demise of feudalism."

ask: What was the cartaz and why did Indian Ocean traders resent it?
book: world-history-2
pages: 48
why: Read printed page 48: from 1502 the Portuguese demanded "that ships trading
  in the Indian Ocean carry a cartaz, a document bearing the Christian cross".
  Ships without one "had their cargoes seized and were sunk", and "Muslim,
  Jewish, and Hindu captains objected to having to bear a document with the
  Christian cross".

ask: Why did Europeans begin searching for new sea routes to Asia?

ask: What happened during the 2008 financial crisis?
book: us-history
pages: 896
why: Read printed page 896: "The prestigious investment firm Lehman Brothers was
  completely wiped out in September 2008", and "the chairman of the Federal
  Reserve, Ben Bernanke, authorized a bailout of the Wall Street firm Bear
  Stearns". The page also covers adjustable-rate loans and foreclosures.
  ADDED 2026-08-10 because this question produced a grounded-looking but FALSE
  answer. Retrieval returned this page together with printed 699 (the Great
  Depression key-terms glossary, including "Black Tuesday October 29, 1929")
  and printed 679 (the causes of the 1929 crash), and the model merged all
  three into a single account of 2008 — "a massive stock sell-off in late
  October", a 40 percent fall, bank runs, the role of automobiles. Every word
  came from the textbook and every claim carried a citation.
  Keep this entry even though it is an easy retrieval hit. It is here as a
  standing reminder that hit rate does not measure grounding: retrieval scores
  a HIT while the answer is wrong by seventy-nine years.
